# Can a WireGuard endpoint get a free public UDP port?

Yes, at ten or more providers, and the reason is arithmetic rather than
generosity. A WireGuard endpoint listens on **one UDP port**. It does not need a
hostname, a path, a certificate or a shared edge. So any service that forwards a
single UDP port is a WireGuard ingress provider, and the question stops being
"who speaks WireGuard" and becomes "which free UDP forward carries a terabyte a
month".

⭐ **That reframing is the finding.** The tunnel market is organised around HTTP,
because HTTP is what most people expose. A WireGuard endpoint needs the smallest
possible unit of that market, and several providers give it away because their
cost to carry one UDP port is close to zero.

The test, applied to every row below:

| test | passes when |
| --- | --- |
| I | a public address and port reach a service the reader runs, and UDP is carried |
| W | a `.conf` is issued, a registration API yields one, an extractor derives one, or the provider's UDP transport carries the datagram |
| V | 1 TiB/month or more is free, or the provider publishes no byte cap and no meter |

⭐ **1 TiB/month is 33.3 GiB/day, which is 3.24 Mbit/s sustained.** The volume bar
is low and the rate bar is not: a provider that caps a free tunnel at 10 Mbit/s
still clears 3.2 TB/month, so a speed cap is rarely what fails V.

⚠ **A vendor's "1 TB" is 0.909 TiB.** It is 9% short of this test, and the table
prints which side of the line each row sits on rather than rounding it up.

The rows above are hosted except the last, which is a rented machine on a free
tier. The three cheapest paths in one sentence: **ROUTE64 for a routed prefix
with no meter, portwarp for a single UDP port with no stated cap, and TCPShield
for a documented 1 TB/month with a scrubbing edge.**

⚠ **"No published cap" is a documented absence and not a quota.** It means no
number exists to design against, and it can end without notice. The three rows
that publish a number are Oracle at 9.09 TiB, TCPShield at 0.909 TiB and
Hoppy at 0.909 TiB on a paid plan.

## What a free row actually looks like

The table hides a difference that decides usability, so each row is stated here
with the thing that ends it.

### ROUTE64

A non-profit tunnel broker on AS212895 that routes an IPv6 `/56` to an endpoint
over WireGuard, GRE, IP6IP or 6in4, with a self-service portal that generates the
configuration (`documented`, read 2026-10-23). "Traffic is not metered", and the
service is designed for 200 Mbit/s, which is 2.16 TB/day.

What ends it: the prefix is IPv6, so a reader without IPv6 cannot reach it, and
the project reserves the right to throttle without naming a threshold. No byte
counter exists at 1 TiB/month.

### ProxyLink

One WireGuard peer on the network edge covers the whole LAN, and any TCP or UDP
service is published on `tcp.proxylink.dev` (`documented`, read 2026-10-23).
Early access is free with no card and "no usage limits except those noted below",
and the only noted limit is 200 MB of recording storage.

⛔ **The same vendor publishes the end of that arrangement, and a re-read found
it.** The plan grid gives Free "3 proxy links (HTTP/HTTPS only)" and "No tunnels",
puts TCP behind the Developer plan at EUR 17/month, and its FAQ says "TCP links,
browser SSH/RDP/VNC, and LAN access all require a tunnel". The record moved from
`qualifies-with-caveat` to `trial-only` because the free path has a published end
and a published price.

What ends it: the early-access period. A reader who builds on it today is building
on a trial.

### 6Project

Five tunnel protocols including WireGuard, a routed IPv6 prefix, and no traffic
allowance on any page read (`inferred`, read 2026-10-23). The rung is `inferred`
because availability depends on the point of presence and the page does not state
a quota in a sentence that survives quoting.

What ends it: the request form is the only channel, and prefixes carry annual
validity with a grace period before deprovisioning.

### portwarp

One active tunnel, two extra ports, TCP and UDP, and "Every plan includes TCP,
UDP, encrypted tunnels, and unlimited bandwidth" (`documented`, read 2026-10-23).
The address is shown in the dashboard and the port is a stored property of the
tunnel, so it survives a reconnect.

What ends it: nothing countable is published, so the first failure is an
unpublished tolerance on a single relay. A UDP port for a WireGuard endpoint is
the product's normal use, which is why this row is stronger than its rung.

### playit.gg

A global anycast relay with a free tier that carries game presets and custom UDP,
and an operator post that calls 1 to 2 TB/day on a free account tolerable
(`reported`, read 2026-10-23). Generic TCP moved to the paid tier; UDP did not.

What ends it: a tolerance rather than a quota, and the same post says the
tolerance moves with regional bandwidth cost.

### portmap.io

One configuration, one forwarding rule, a random external port, and "Unlimited
bandwidth" as a product line (`documented`, read 2026-10-23). The transport is
OpenVPN rather than WireGuard, so the UDP port carries an OpenVPN session and a
WireGuard endpoint can run inside it.

What ends it: the free plan's port is random and a custom external port is paid,
so the endpoint moves whenever the tunnel is recreated.

### NetBird reverse proxy

An L4 proxy that publishes TCP and UDP resources on a `*.proxy.netbird.io`
address, on a free plan of 5 users and 100 machines (`documented`, read
2026-10-23). The data plane is WireGuard, but the key never leaves the client machine, so
a raw `.conf` cannot reproduce it.

What ends it: no byte allowance is published and the vendor describes the proxy
as shared and best effort.

### TCPShield Sentry Tunnel

The documented number in this class: the free plan carries "1.0 TB monthly
bandwidth", one network and three domains, and the Sentry tunnel forwards any TCP
or UDP over VXLAN from a scrubbing edge to a host the reader runs (`inferred`,
read 2026-10-23). The rung is `inferred` because the pricing page states the byte
allowance for the base plan and no page states that Sentry is included in it.

What ends it: 1.0 TB/month is 0.909 TiB, and crossing it is undocumented. The
origin needs a Linux host with root to create the VXLAN interface.

### openport.pw

One persistent port with no signup, UDP enabled by default (`documented`, read
2026-10-23). It is the only free row here that needs no identity at all.

What ends it: no byte allowance and no rate are published, and the port belongs to
a single-operator service.

### The public frps services

Three services publish an open frp server: freefrp.com on ports 10000 to 60000
with a 30 Mbit/s sustained ceiling, freefrp.net on 10001 to 50000, and the
Chinese panels OpenFrp and MeFrp at 10 to 12 Mbit/s with a daily traffic grant
(`documented` and `reported`, read 2026-10-23). All four carry UDP through `frpc`,
which makes them the largest free UDP surface found.

⚠ **These are public relays run by volunteers, and their capacity is the
operator's.** freefrp.com states that sustained traffic at or above 30 Mbit/s
gets a `/24` blocked, which is a rate and not a quota. 1 TiB/month needs only
3.24 Mbit/s, so the rate does not bind, and nothing published says what does.

### Oracle Always Free

The one row with a documented allowance large enough to stop worrying: 10 TB per
month of outbound transfer, "one VNIC with one public IP address", and a free AMD
micro instance that is an ordinary Linux VM (`documented`, read 2026-10-23). Run
`wg-quick` on it and every UDP port is reachable.

What ends it: a card at signup, and idle instances are reclaimed after 7 days of
low use. 10 TB is 9.09 TiB, which is nine times the test.

## What breaks first

Three shapes, and they are not interchangeable.

1. **A published quota.** TCPShield at 1.0 TB and Nekotopia at 50 GB. This is the
   only shape a plan can be designed against, because the ceiling is known.
2. **A published rate.** ROUTE64 at 200 Mbit/s, OpenFrp at 12 Mbit/s, freefrp.com
   at 30 Mbit/s sustained. A rate does not bound a month, so 1 TiB is reachable
   on every one of these and the number tells a reader nothing about volume.
3. ⚠ **An unpublished tolerance.** portwarp, playit.gg, portmap.io, NetBird. This
   is worse than a quota because there is no threshold at which the path is safe,
   and it is the shape most free rows have.

## Chaining

**The managed chain.** `ROUTE64 `/56` or portwarp UDP port -> `wg-quick` on any
host the reader already runs`. The scarce link is IPv6 reachability for the first
and an unpublished tolerance for the second. Nothing in the chain is paid.

**The free-VM chain.** `Oracle Always Free -> wg-quick -> a UDP listener`. The
scarce link is the card at signup, and 9.09 TiB/month is nine times the test.

**The documented-allowance chain.** `TCPShield Sentry -> VXLAN -> a root Linux
host -> a WireGuard listener inside the tunnel`. The scarce link is the root host,
which is a machine outside this chain, so this chain needs the Oracle row to exist.

⚠ **The chain that does not exist.** A free managed service that issues a
WireGuard `.conf` and forwards raw UDP on a free tier. Serveo issues the `.conf`
and charges for the UDP. 21tunnel advertised all three and its control plane does
not resolve.

## What this document does not know

- **No free UDP forward was measured end to end.** Every qualifying provider
  allocates a port only after an account exists, and no account was created. Every
  volume figure here is a vendor claim or a forum report.
- **Port stability across a real reconnect is documented for four services**
  (portwarp, playit.gg, pinggy, openport.pw) and untested for the rest.
- **The free UDP rate was never measured.** The only measured rate in this topic
  is 26.9 KiB/s on Pinggy's HTTP tier, which is not the UDP path.
